# Competitor research: admin work agents pay to hand off (2026-11-06)

Asked by Ismael after dropping AI made content: for the three admin jobs most likely worth
$99 to $199 a month (contract to close, seller updates, buyer agreements and fees), who already
does it, what they charge, whether they use AI, what they do well and badly, and where Hopkins
could win. Most figures come from vendor pages and vendor written comparisons, so treat prices
as "what the page said on 2026-11-06", not audited facts. Sources at the end of each section.

## 1. Contract to close (transaction coordination)

What agents pay now for a person to do it: a freelance transaction coordinator is about $300 to
$600 per closed file ($400 to $500 most common in 2026 guides); licensed ones $750 to $1,000; a
full time coordinator $45,000 to $60,000 a year.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| dotloop (Zillow) | Market leader for documents, e-sign and deal "loops" | Some document analysis mentioned in one source; mostly not AI | Free up to 10 deals, Premium $31.99/mo, teams custom | Cheap, huge adoption, 4.7 on Capterra (673 reviews) | Data goes through Zillow; other agents still email PDFs; feels like paperwork software |
| SkySlope | Compliance first platform for brokerages | Compliance checks; little AI | Starts about $25/mo for agents, suites far higher | Audit trail, brokerages love it | Hard to use per some reviews (4.0 on Capterra), few integrations |

## 2. Seller updates (weekly report to the seller)

| Product | What it is | AI | Price | Good | Bad |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| ListingAI | Listing performance reports from portal views and saves | Yes | Not checked | Client ready updates | Only what portals report |

Read: crowded, and mostly cheap or free. The part that matters (portal views) has no open API,
and the best funded new player (Beacon) has the industry's best known marketers behind it.
Weak as a product of its own at $99 to $199; fine as a feature inside something bigger.

## 3. Buyer agreements and the fee conversation (since August 2024)

The rule: since 17 August 2024 an MLS agent needs a written buyer agreement before touring, with
the pay stated clearly; pay is negotiable and can't be offered on the MLS. States differ:
California (AB 2992) widened it from 1 January 2026 and caps first terms at 90 days; Alabama
moved signing to before the offer, not the tour; Colorado's regulator got complaints about how
agreements were presented.

| Product | What it does | AI | Price |
|---|---|---|---|
| dotloop, SkySlope, zipForm (Lone Wolf), DocuSign | Where the agreement itself is filled and signed, from the brokerage's or association's approved forms | Little | As above |
| Jointly | Transaction software that drafts and tracks buyer agreements and e-signs | Not stated | Free sign up, plans not shown |
| Highnote | Interactive buyer presentations with open tracking, pitched at justifying the fee | "AI" mentioned | 14 day trial, price not shown |
| Ylopo buyer presentation | Buyer slides with live market data and the agent's value | Not stated | Part of Ylopo, price not shown |
| MoxiWorks (MoxiPresent), Cloud CMA (Lone Wolf) | Buyer presentations from MLS data | Little | Bundled with brokerage suites |
| Jtek | CRM at $60/mo that stores each signed agreement on the buyer | Some | $60/mo |
| Etsy templates | Canva buyer consultation packs about pay after the settlement | No | A few dollars |

Real pain seen: agents on r/realtors describe buyers going quiet the moment they're asked to sign,
and the advice is to raise it in the first consultation. No dedicated product owns this yet, but
the agreement itself has to be the brokerage's approved form, so software can't write it; it can
only help win the signature, track terms and expiries, and keep the pay clear.

## Where Hopkins could win, honestly

None of the three is an open field. Ranked for a solo founder selling through Instagram and DMs:

1. **Contract to close for the agent themselves**, with the buyer agreement at the start and the
   weekly seller or buyer update built in. The only one of the three where agents already spend
   hundreds per deal. The opening: incumbents aren't AI native, and the AI natives are built for
   coordinators and brokerages. Hopkins would be the agent's own deal assistant, used from a
   chat and their phone: forward the contract, it builds the timeline, chases the lender, title
   and the other agent from the agent's own inbox with one tap approval, texts the agent what's
   due, and sends the client a plain weekly update. Why agents would switch: cheaper than a
   person after one deal a month, no dashboard to learn, it does the chasing instead of
   listing tasks. Risks: ListedKit and Nekst are close already; accuracy on dates is everything.
2. **Seller updates**: only as a free part of 1.
3. **Buyer agreements**: only as the first step of 1 (signature tracking, term and expiry
   reminders, clear pay), never as its own product, because the form belongs to the brokerage.

Pricing if 1 is built: per active deal is how this market prices (ListedKit, Paperless Pipeline,
Open to Close), anchored against $400 per file for a person. A fair start: first deal free;
Solo $99/mo up to 5 active deals; Pro $149/mo up to 15; Team $299/mo up to 40 with extra seats;
$29 per extra deal. One price for everyone would undercharge busy agents and scare off part
timers.

The legal standard
Section 101(a)(15)(O)(i) of the Immigration and Nationality Act provides a nonimmigrant
classification for a person who has extraordinary ability in the sciences, arts, education,
business or athletics, demonstrated by sustained national or international acclaim (8
U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15)(O)(i)). The O-1A classification covers extraordinary ability in the
sciences, education, business or athletics (8 CFR 214.2(o)(1)(ii)(A)(1)). Extraordinary
ability in the field of science, education, business or athletics means "a level of expertise
indicating that the person is one of the small percentage who have arisen to the very top
of the field of endeavor" (8 CFR 214.2(o)(3)(ii)).
A petitioner may establish eligibility in one of two ways. The first is evidence of a major,
internationally recognised award (8 CFR 214.2(o)(3)(iii)(A)). The second is evidence
meeting at least three of the eight criteria (8 CFR 214.2(o)(3)(iii)(B)(1) to (8)). Where a
criterion does not readily apply to the occupation, comparable evidence may be
submitted (8 CFR 214.2(o)(3)(iii)(C)).
USCIS reviews the evidence in two steps (USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 2, Part M, Ch. 4).
[Attorney: confirm the current Policy Manual section and the 8 CFR 214.2(o)(6)(iii) pin
cite.] It first decides whether the evidence meets the regulatory criteria. It then reviews
all the evidence together in a final merits determination, to decide whether the
beneficiary has sustained national or international acclaim and is among the small
percentage at the very top of the field (USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 2, Part M, Ch. 4). The
standard of proof is a preponderance of the evidence (Matter of Chawathe, 25 I&N Dec.
369, 375–76 (AAO 2010)).
The petition must also include a written advisory opinion from an appropriate peer group,
a copy of any written contract or a summary of an oral agreement, and an explanation of
the events or activities, with an itinerary where needed (8 CFR 214.2(o)(2)(ii)(B) to (D); 8
CFR 214.2(o)(5)).

Original contributions of major significance: 8 CFR 214.2(o)(3)(iii)

Dr. Halász created FoldSight, an open-source method for predicting how drug binding
pockets change shape (FoldSight article, client file pp. 33–34). An independent expert
writes that it turned a prediction that took weeks of simulation for each protein into one
that takes minutes (Letter of Prof. Lucia Ferrante, client file p. 27).
The method and her role. FoldSight is a graph neural network, trained on 14,000 molecular
dynamics trajectories, that predicts from a single static structure the range of shapes a
binding pocket can take, in under five minutes on one graphics card (FoldSight article,
client file p. 33). The article's author contribution statement says that Dr. Halász
conceived the method, wrote the software, ran all experiments and wrote the paper, and
that Prof. Marchetti supervised the work and edited the paper (FoldSight article, client
file p. 34). Prof. Marchetti, her postdoctoral supervisor and co-author, confirms that Dr.
Halász conceived the core idea and wrote most of the code, and that her own role was
supervision and funding (Letter of Prof. Elena Marchetti, client file p. 29).
Measured results. On a held-out test set of 310 proteins, FoldSight recovered 83 percent
of the cryptic pockets seen in long simulations, against 41 percent for the best previous
method (FoldSight article, client file p. 33). Its median run time was 4.2 minutes per
protein (FoldSight article, client file p. 34). [Attorney: verify these figures against the
publisher's copy, to be obtained.]
